# Den Gear Design Guide

Oct 5, 2026 · @Rusty Pines. Stored by Firefly on Oct 6 as GrumpyDingo gave it, laid out as Markdown, with the wording kept. To become a skill once the basic pieces are settled. The factions this guide's styles will hang on are in `DEN-FACTIONS.md`.

## How to use this guide

Build every piece symmetric first, then make one deliberate break pass: a notch, a charm, a chipped edge. Symmetry makes a piece look intended; the single break gives it character and a focal point. The rest of this guide is what decides where that break goes, how big it is, and how the piece fits its set and tier.

The passes, in order:

1. **Symmetry pass.** Build the mirrored structure: band, plates, socket. Even spacing, even gaps.
2. **Silhouette pass.** Fill it solid at game size. It must read as its slot and its style from the outline alone.
3. **Break pass.** Add one asymmetric element on purpose, sized as a small accent. Never two competing breaks.
4. **Value pass.** Greyscale it on the coat. Light side, shadow side, and the gem as the strongest contrast.
5. **Tier pass.** Check it against the tier budget: parts, gems, glow.
6. **Motion pass.** Watch it through the full stride on both dogs.

The game sections below are what five shipped games did and said. The recommendations section turns it into rules for the Den.

## Core principles

Twelve principles decide most of whether a piece reads well; symmetry is one of them, not all of them.

| # | Principle | What it means | Den example |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Silhouette first | The solid black outline at game size must name the slot, style and tier | A cuff and a bracelet must differ in outline, not just gem count |
| 2 | Big, medium, small | One dominant shape, a few secondary, small accents; about 60/30/10 visual weight | Band, socket, rivets and glint |
| 3 | Symmetry, broken on purpose | Mirrored structure plus one intended break; near-symmetry reads as a mistake | A symmetric collar with one off-centre tag |
| 4 | Shape language | Rounds friendly, squares sturdy, triangles dangerous; one dominant per set | Hunter set: swept points lying with the coat |
| 5 | No tangents | Edges either overlap clearly or leave a clear gap, never almost touch | Cuff edge vs sleeve hem |
| 6 | Value before colour | Readable in greyscale, and separate from the tan and cream coat | Gold sinking into a tan coat |
| 7 | Rest areas | Cluster detail near the focal point; leave quiet areas | Stitching near the gem, plain band elsewhere |
| 8 | Construction logic | Visible hinges, straps, rivets; respects how a dog moves | Nothing rigid across the elbow or stifle crease |
| 9 | Tier budget | Higher tiers add parts, gems, silhouette and glow; low tiers stay simple | Saturation and glow reserved for gems and rarity |
| 10 | Set motif | One distinctive shape repeats across every slot of a set | The same notch on collar, cuffs and tail rings |
| 11 | Odds and rhythm | Three gives a centre, two splits the eye; spacing even or evenly progressing | Tail plates shrinking toward the tip |
| 12 | Reads in motion | Checked mid-stride; secondary motion adds life | A swinging charm on the bracelet |

## Diablo IV

Diablo IV's rule is that the game camera sets the detail budget: big shapes on armor, and anything that hurts readability gets cut.

**What the art director said.** Art director John Mueller said everything in Diablo serves the isometric camera. The team favours bigger shapes on armor and reduces whatever hurts readability, aiming for a detail level that stays readable but grounded (Destructoid). On mood, his line was that "darkness does not mean bleakness": because players spend thousands of hours in the game, colour is used deliberately rather than everything being grim (PCGamesN). The reference was oil-style fantasy painting, not photography; the goal was something that feels painted, not realism.

**The system.** Every armor piece (helmet, chest, gloves, legs, boots) is dyeable separately, and hundreds of componentized armor pieces had to work across body types and five classes (Destructoid).

**Vibe and shapes.** Gothic, medieval and Renaissance-painting dark fantasy. Heavy, grounded materials; large plates; few small fiddly bits.

**Lessons for the Den**
- Design at game size first. If a detail disappears there, it was never part of the design.
- Fewer, bigger shapes. A cuff is a band and a socket, not a band covered in tiny ornaments.
- Material swaps must not break the read. Diablo's dyes are the same problem as the Smithy's materials: the silhouette and values carry the piece, the material only recolours it.
- Dark or earthy is fine, but keep colour purposeful; reserve saturation for the gem.

## Guild Wars 2

Guild Wars 2's lesson is that top-tier items earn their status with effects that serve the piece, and with one theme repeated across a whole family of items.

**What the art director said.** Daniel Dociu explained that the painterly look was chosen by playing to the team's strength (strong concept painters) instead of competing on raw production budget. The hand-crafted quality was then pushed everywhere: cinematics, UI, branding and in-game assets, unified as one style (Tap-Repeatedly). He also wanted modelers to build on a concept, not just copy it.

**What the legendary team said.** For the End of Dragons legendaries, visual effects artist Nathaniel Hubbell set four needs (GuildWars2.com):
1. Effects capture the theme the weapon is built on.
2. Effects showcase the weapon and never hide it; in his words, legendary weapons, "not legendary particles".
3. Balance spectacle with finesse, so it feels rare but stays readable in a chaotic fight.
4. Look good against every background in the world.

He also kept bright hit-flashes short, and had designers limit how often the big kill effect plays, so it could be larger without becoming constant noise. Mixing saturated glowing parts with solid parts that take the world's light helped effects fit any scene.

**The family idea.** Prop artist Chelsea Mills described one base Aurene set plus five variants per weapon, 96 appearances in all (GuildWars2.com). Within a set, weapons share auras, footfall effects and a kill animation (GW2 Wiki), so the set reads as one theme in motion, not just in shape.

**Vibe and shapes.** Painterly, high fantasy, bright where it counts. Legendaries are big, distinctive silhouettes with a single strong idea (crystal, lightning, ice), backed by matching auras and trails.

**Lessons for the Den**
- Rarity glow and infusion accents should frame the piece, never cover it. Test them on sand, pink, black and blue, like the bench already does.
- Keep the brightest flash short. A rare-item glow can pulse briefly rather than glow constantly.
- Give each set one theme that also lives in its effects: matching paw-print trails, a shared idle shimmer.
- Play to the Den's strength: the flat two-tone vector style. Push it everywhere (icons, UI, bench) instead of chasing a rendered look.

## Elder Scrolls Online

ESO's lesson is to separate style from material: a style (motif) is a culture's shape language, applied to any slot and any material, which is almost exactly the Den's planned set template.

**What the art director said.** CJ Grebb's base rule is to keep the world grounded in reality and treat it as old and lived-in; real-world cultures inspire each region, and imagination is layered on top of that realism (The AU Review). For new Daedric work, the team started from the original game's shape language, then asked which new shapes and materials fit, and kept checking whether each design still looked like it belonged. Concept artist Joe Watmough built Mehrunes Dagon's armor from the layered, blade-like Daedric shapes, and adjusted the face so he would not be confused with a similar character.

**The system.** Weapons and armor are crafted in a style using a matching style material, and each character starts knowing their own culture's style (UESP). Every motif covers every slot, so a whole outfit can be one culture. Variants (a base style plus a recoloured or reworked sibling) stretch one design into two.

**Vibe and shapes.** Grounded, historical, cultural. Each culture has its own unmistakable shape vocabulary: Daedric layered blades, Nord fur and knotwork, Dwemer brass gears.

**Lessons for the Den**
- Treat a style as a shape vocabulary, not a decoration. The Den's code digits already separate material from style; keep it that way.
- Write each style's vocabulary down in a short list (edge profile, notch shape, plate count, gem cut) so every slot draws from it.
- Ground fantasy in something real. For dingo gear, real working-dog and hunting gear (harness leather, brass buckles, bone and antler) is the bedrock.
- Check distinctiveness against nearby styles, like the Dagon face fix: two styles should never be mistakable at game size.

## Borderlands

Borderlands' lesson is that shape should promise behaviour: a player should know roughly how an item works before reading its stats, because each maker has a look that matches its mechanic.

**What the art directors said.** Borderlands 4 art director Adam May said players should recognise a manufacturer on pickup and roughly know how its weapon will behave. Art and design go back and forth so the look matches the mechanics: fast-firing guns get sleek, angular, aggressive shapes; guns firing giant rockets get big, round, bulbous ones (Insider Gaming). Borderlands 2 art director Jeramy Cooke gave the visual codes: camo always means Dahl, Maliwan has a blue glow and sporty orange stripes, Bandit has graffiti and red, Hyperion is always yellow, Jakobs is old-timey wood (GodisaGeek).

**The system.** Guns are built from interchangeable parts; Borderlands 2 went from about 5 or 6 parts per gun to about 12 to 14, and scopes became parameterized instead of a handful of static pieces (Kotaku). Cooke also said the style found its voice when they switched from realism to the look of their own concept art.

**Vibe and shapes.** Hand-inked concept-art style, bold outlines, loud brand colours, humour. Each maker owns a shape family and a colour code, applied to every part.

**Lessons for the Den**
- Make shape match function. A swift set gets swept, angular points; a guard set gets broad rounded plates.
- Give each style or maker one unmistakable visual code (a colour stripe, a wood inlay, a notch) that appears on every slot.
- Parts-based building is what the Den's code digits already do. Keep each part's silhouette distinct so random combinations still read.
- Borderlands chose its style by matching its own concept art. The Den's flat, outlined look is the same move: lean in, don't apologise for it.

## Path of Exile

Path of Exile gives the most usable design method of the five: start from a plain base, stack themes on it, and decide up front whether a piece should be broad (blends into a set) or iconic (recognisable from far away).

**What the concept artist said.** In a talk summarised on the official site, concept artist George Lovesy noted that a sword must always have a handle and a blade, yet the game needs dozens of variations (pathofexile.com). His answer is theme-stacking: accept that no idea is fully original, start from a generic base (a plain European armor), then layer themes until it becomes something new. Materials are themes too: space, crystal, fire and stone each turn the same sword into a different item.

**Broad, iconic, hybrid.** Lovesy sorts every design into three kinds:

| Kind | Test | Use it for |
|---|---|---|
| Iconic | Recognisable from far away; removing a key part changes what it is | Bosses, legendary or signature pieces |
| Broad | Fits the world without standing out; any piece can be removed | Pieces meant to mix with other sets |
| Hybrid-iconic | One iconic part (often the helmet), the rest broad | Sets with one signature slot |

He added that a world mixing realism and fantasy calls for both broad and iconic designs, while simpler, more stylised games lean iconic, with simplified shapes, themes and colours.

**Item art.** At ExileCon, senior 3D artist Dane Brennand and designer Hrishikesh Sidhartha said every item gets its own art treatment, and even items with the same stats look different so each is instantly recognisable (Gamers Unchained).

**Vibe and shapes.** Grounded, dark and gritty, worn and historical, with flashes of the bizarre on uniques.

**Lessons for the Den**
- When stuck, theme-stack: base cuff, then add one theme at a time (antler, then moon, then frost) until it feels new.
- Decide broad or iconic before drawing. Most Den gear should be broad so pieces mix across sets; one slot per set (likely the collar) can be the iconic one.
- The Den's flat stylised look sits on the iconic end, so keep shapes, themes and colours simple and strong.
- Run the removal test: take the signature part away and see if the piece is still itself.

## Patterns across all five

All five games agree on six things, even though their looks could hardly differ more.

| Pattern | Diablo IV | Guild Wars 2 | ESO | Borderlands | Path of Exile |
|---|---|---|---|---|---|
| Readability at play size rules | Big shapes for the camera | Effects readable in chaos | Distinct at a glance | Know the maker on pickup | Iconic means readable far away |
| Style is a shape vocabulary | Gothic, painted | Painterly, bold themes | A motif per culture | A shape family per maker | Theme-stacked bases |
| Material is separate from shape | Dye system | Variant sets of one weapon | Style material per motif | Brand colour codes | Materials as themes |
| Grounded first, fantasy layered on | Painting, not photo | Strong concept-art base | Reality, then imagination | Concept-art look | Gritty base, bizarre uniques |
| Effects serve the item | Purposeful colour | "Not legendary particles" | Effects art per release | Elemental glow as code | Unique art per item |
| Shape signals function or role | Heavy reads heavy | Theme drives silhouette | Culture drives silhouette | Fast is angular, rockets bulbous | Broad vs iconic by role |

None of them treats symmetry as the goal. Symmetry is the scaffold they all build on, and the identity comes from the vocabulary, the theme stack and one signature feature.

## Recommendations for the Den

Keep symmetry as the law for structure, and make the break pass a designed step with its own rules: one break per piece, small, placed at the focal point, and repeated across the set so it becomes the set's signature.

### The break pass, in detail

There are three kinds of break; pick one per piece.

| Kind | What it is | Den example |
|---|---|---|
| Subtractive | A notch, chip or cut taken out of a symmetric edge | One V-notch on the cuff's upper edge |
| Additive | A small part hung or fixed off-centre | A charm on the bracelet, a tag on the collar |
| Material | One inlay or plate in a different material | One antler plate among metal tail guards |

Rules for placing it:

1. **One break only.** Two breaks compete and the piece reads as messy.
2. **Small.** The break is the 10% accent in big, medium, small, never the dominant shape.
3. **At the focal point.** Put it near the gem or on the near side facing the camera; the dog faces right, so that's the side players see.
4. **Away from joints and seams.** A notch at the elbow or the sleeve hem reads as a fitting error, not a design choice.
5. **Repeat it across the set.** The same notch on collar, cuffs and tail rings is what makes a set read as a set (ESO motif, Borderlands brand code).
6. **Clearly deliberate.** A break must be large enough to read at game size; anything smaller than about one contour line weight looks like a mistake.

### A style sheet per set

Write each style down before drawing it, like an ESO motif:

| Field | Example: Hunter | Example: Warden |
|---|---|---|
| Shape language | Swept points along the coat | Broad rounded plates |
| Signature break | Back-swept V-notch | Round stud off-centre |
| Edge profile | Bevelled, tapered ends | Thick rolled rim |
| Gem cut | Narrow diamond | Round cabochon |
| Plate count | Odd, shrinking toward the tip | Odd, even sizes |
| Role | Speed, reads light and angular | Defence, reads heavy and round |

The last row is Borderlands' rule: shape promises behaviour.

### Broad or iconic per slot

Make most slots broad so pieces mix freely across sets. Give each set exactly one iconic slot, the one players recognise across a field: the collar is the natural choice, since it sits on the head-and-neck read. That is Path of Exile's hybrid-iconic set.

### Tier budget

| Tier | Parts | Gems | Break | Effect |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Common | Base shape only | None | None | None |
| Uncommon | Base plus one secondary | 1 small | Optional | None |
| Rare | Full big, medium, small | 1 socketed | Yes | Brief glint |
| Epic | Adds a layered plate | 1 large or 3 small | Yes | Soft pulse |
| Legendary | Iconic silhouette | Signature gem | Yes, plus set motif | Short-lived idle effect, set-wide trail |

Keep the brightest effect brief, as Guild Wars 2 does, and never let it cover the piece.

### Grounding for dingo gear

Take the real base from working and hunting dog gear: harness leather, brass buckles and rings, stitched edges, bone and antler. Then theme-stack on top: moon, frost, eucalyptus, desert. It keeps the fantasy believable on a canine body.

## Checks the Den Lens could run

Most of these principles can become numbers, so lint catches them before a human eye has to.

| Check | What it measures | Flags when |
|---|---|---|
| Symmetry score | Overlap of the piece with its own mirror image | Between about 85% and 98%: accidentally lopsided |
| Break count | Asymmetric regions left after mirroring | More than one break per piece |
| Silhouette distinctiveness | Overlap between solid silhouettes of two pieces or styles | Two styles or tiers are near-identical in outline |
| Tangents | Gap between separate edges | Closer than one contour weight without overlapping |
| Value contrast | Greyscale contrast of piece vs the coat under it | Piece sinks into the coat on any material |
| Detail density | Edge density heatmap | Detail spread evenly, no rest areas |
| Game-size survival | Drawn width of the thinnest line at game scale | Detail or break vanishes below about one pixel |
| Tier budget | Part, gem and effect counts per code | A piece exceeds or falls short of its tier row |
| Effect coverage | Fraction of the piece hidden by glow or particles | Effect hides more than a small share of the piece |
